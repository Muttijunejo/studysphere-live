The Human Respiratory System — Breathing and Gas Exchange

General Science Respiratory System 10 Practice MCQs

You breathe about 20,000 times a day without thinking about it. These notes follow the air you breathe in — from your nose to the tiny air sacs in your lungs — and explain how oxygen reaches your blood and carbon dioxide leaves it. 1. Why Do We Breathe?

Every cell of your body needs oxygen to release energy from food. That chemical process is called respiration. When cells use oxygen, they make carbon dioxide as a waste gas, which the body must throw out. Breathing is the job of bringing oxygen in and sending carbon dioxide out. Roman Urdu: Saans lene ka kaam oxygen andar lana aur carbon dioxide bahar nikalna hai — har cell ko khane se energy nikalne ke liye oxygen chahiye.

Breathing is not the same as respiration. Breathing is the mechanical movement of air in and out of the lungs. Respiration is the chemical process inside the cells that releases energy from food using oxygen. Tests love to ask this difference — remember it.

The air we breathe in is about 21% oxygen and about 78% nitrogen. The air we breathe out has less oxygen (about 16%) and more carbon dioxide (about 4%) — the rest is mostly nitrogen, which the body does not use.

2. The Path of Air — From Nose to Lungs

Air follows one fixed path into the lungs. Learn it in order — tests ask "which comes first" and "which comes after the trachea" questions from this list.

StepPartWhat it does
1Nose (nostrils)Air enters here. Tiny hairs and sticky mucus trap dust and germs; the air is warmed and moistened.
2Pharynx (throat)A common passage for air and food.
3Larynx (voice box)Contains the vocal cords — this is where your voice is made.
4Trachea (windpipe)A tube about 10–12 cm long, kept open by C-shaped rings of cartilage.
5BronchiThe trachea divides into two bronchi — one going to each lung.
6BronchiolesThe bronchi divide again and again into smaller and smaller tubes.
7Alveoli (air sacs)Tiny sacs at the ends of the bronchioles — this is where gas exchange happens.

The epiglottis is a small flap of cartilage that covers the windpipe when you swallow, so food goes down the food pipe (oesophagus) and not into the lungs. Roman Urdu: Nigalte waqt epiglottis naam ki chhoti si parat saans ki nali ko band kar deti hai, taa ke khana ghalat rasta na le.

Test trap: do not mix up the two pipes — the trachea carries air to the lungs, the oesophagus carries food to the stomach.

3. The Alveoli — Where Gas Exchange Happens

The alveoli are about 300 million tiny air sacs in the two lungs — like millions of tiny balloons. Their walls are only one cell thick, and each one is wrapped in a net of tiny blood vessels called capillaries. This thin wall is what makes gas exchange possible.

Gas exchange happens by diffusion — gases move from where there is more of them to where there is less:

  • Oxygen moves from the air in the alveoli into the blood. In the blood it joins haemoglobin in the red blood cells, which carry it to every part of the body.
  • Carbon dioxide moves from the blood into the alveoli, and is breathed out with the next breath.

All the alveoli together give a huge surface area — about 70 square metres, roughly the floor of a small house. That big area is the reason gas exchange is so fast. Roman Urdu: Phephron mein taqreeban 30 crore chhoti theliyan (alveoli) hoti hain — in ki deewarein bohat patli hoti hain, is liye oxygen khoon mein aur carbon dioxide bahar aasani se guzar jati hai.

The right lung is slightly bigger than the left lung, and it has three lobes while the left lung has two lobes (the heart takes up space on the left side).

4. The Diaphragm — The Main Breathing Muscle

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped sheet of muscle that sits below the lungs and separates the chest from the abdomen. It is the main muscle of breathing. The muscles between the ribs (intercostal muscles) help it.

Breathing in (inhalation): the diaphragm contracts and flattens (moves down), and the ribs move up and out. The chest cavity becomes bigger, the air pressure inside the lungs falls, and air rushes in from outside.

Breathing out (exhalation): the diaphragm relaxes and domes up, and the ribs move down and in. The chest cavity becomes smaller, the pressure rises, and air is pushed out.

Normal breathing in is active work, but normal breathing out is mostly passive — the muscles simply relax. Roman Urdu: Saans andar lete waqt diaphragm (saans ka main muscle) neeche ki taraf chapta ho jata hai aur seena phailta hai; saans bahar nikalte waqt woh wapas upar gumbad ki shakal mein aa jata hai.

Test trick: if a question asks what happens to the diaphragm when you breathe in, the answer is always it contracts and flattens / moves down.

5. Breathing Rate and Lung Capacity

  • A healthy adult at rest breathes about 12–20 times per minute. A newborn baby breathes much faster — about 40 times per minute.
  • During exercise the breathing rate rises — it can go above 40 breaths per minute in hard exercise — because the muscles need more oxygen and make more carbon dioxide.
  • Tidal volume is the air moved in one normal breath — about 500 mL (half a litre).
  • Vital capacity is the maximum air you can breathe out after the deepest possible breath in — about 4.6 litres in an adult man.
  • Total lung capacity is the total air the lungs can hold — about 6 litres. Even after breathing out as hard as you can, about 1 litre of air (residual volume) always stays in the lungs.
  • Breathing is controlled by the brain (the medulla), which watches the carbon dioxide level in the blood — not the oxygen level. When carbon dioxide rises, you feel the need to breathe.

Remember the numbers: 12–20 breaths per minute at rest, 500 mL per normal breath, 4.6 L vital capacity. These three numbers are asked again and again in job tests.

6. Common Diseases of the Respiratory System

  • Asthma — the airways narrow and swell, making breathing difficult. The person wheezes and coughs, often during cold air, dust or exercise.
  • Bronchitis — inflammation of the bronchi, usually with a long-lasting cough. Smoking is a common cause.
  • Pneumonia — an infection of the alveoli, where the air sacs fill with fluid, so less oxygen reaches the blood. It causes fever, cough and difficulty in breathing.
  • Tuberculosis (TB) — caused by the bacteria Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It attacks the lungs, causing a cough that lasts for weeks, fever and weight loss. It spreads through the air when a sick person coughs or sneezes. It is curable with a full course of medicine.
  • Common cold — caused by a virus. It affects the nose and throat, not the lungs, and usually goes away on its own.
  • Smoking damages the alveoli and the cilia that clean the airways. Over time it can cause emphysema (alveoli break down) and greatly increases the risk of lung cancer.

Test trap: TB is caused by bacteria, while the common cold is caused by a virus. Do not mix them up — this is a favourite test question.

Quick Facts — Test Mein Aane Wale Points

  • Path of air: nose → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli.
  • Gas exchange happens in the alveoli by diffusion — oxygen into the blood, carbon dioxide out.
  • About 300 million alveoli; total surface area about 70 m²; walls one cell thick.
  • Oxygen is carried in the blood by haemoglobin in the red blood cells.
  • The diaphragm is the main breathing muscle: it contracts and flattens when you breathe in, and relaxes and domes up when you breathe out.
  • Normal breathing rate at rest: 12–20 breaths per minute. Tidal volume: 500 mL. Vital capacity: about 4.6 L.
  • The epiglottis stops food entering the windpipe. The trachea carries air; the oesophagus carries food.
  • Breathing is controlled by the brain (medulla), which watches the carbon dioxide level in the blood.
  • TB is caused by bacteria (Mycobacterium tuberculosis); the common cold is caused by a virus; asthma narrows the airways.
  • Breathing (air in and out) is NOT the same as respiration (energy release inside cells).
